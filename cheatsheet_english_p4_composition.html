📗 English Cheat Sheet

P4 Composition

The 5-phase story structure, the 2025 top-school theme matrix, and the self-check traps that separate AL1 from AL2/3.

🆕Why P4 Feels Different

Stepping up to Primary 4 English brings a major shift in composition writing. Looking across the 2025 exam papers from top schools like Nanyang, ACS, and Tao Nan, the training wheels are officially off: no more helping words are provided at the bottom of the page, and you are introduced to the PSLE-style 3-picture format.

To score an AL1, you cannot just list what is happening in the pictures one by one. You must use the pictures to tell a cohesive story that is tightly woven around the given theme or value (like honesty or kindness).

📐1. The Master Story Structure

Every top-scoring composition follows a clear narrative curve. Organise your writing into these five distinct phases, matching the classic plot arc used by examiners to grade your work.

1

Exposition: Setting the Scene

Start with an engaging hook instead of a generic introduction. Introduce your main character, establish a vivid setting (where and when), and set a peaceful baseline before the action kicks off.

2

Conflict: The Inciting Incident

Introduce the main problem or an internal dilemma. This is the exact moment where the character faces a choice or something unexpected disrupts their day, triggering the Rising Action.

3

Climax: The Peak Action

This is the highest point of tension in your story. Make it dramatic! Describe the character's physical reactions, racing thoughts, or immediate panic as the problem reaches its absolute worst.

4

Falling Action & Resolution: Solving the Problem

Show the immediate consequences of the climax. How is the problem fixed? Who steps in to help, or what choice does the character make to correct their mistake?

5

Conclusion: Theme-Tied Reflection

Never end abruptly with "Then I went home happily." Wrap up your story by showing how the character changed or what lesson they learned, explicitly connecting it right back to the composition's main theme.

🎯2. 2025 Top-School Theme Matrix

The biggest trap for P4 students is writing a great story but going completely out of point because they ignored the core theme. Below are the most frequent themes tested in the 2025 exam papers, how to plot them, and phrases that win major language marks.

Being Honest

Typical plot: finding an item of value (e.g., a wallet packed with cash at a void deck) and battling the internal temptation to keep it before doing the right thing.

An intense internal tug-of-war raged within me.

My palms sweated as greed reared its ugly head.

Honesty is truly the best policy.

Being Considerate

Typical plot: noticing someone in need (e.g., an elderly lady struggling with heavy bags or a pregnant woman on a crowded MRT train) and sacrificing your own comfort to help.

My heavy school bag weighed me down, but my conscience weighed heavier.

Without a second thought, I gave up my seat.

A warm, fuzzy feeling enveloped my chest.

Being Kind

Typical plot: spotting a classmate or a stranger facing public embarrassment or hardship (e.g., dropping their food tray in the school canteen) and stepping in to comfort them.

A sea of mocking laughter erupted around him.

I sprinted over to extend a helping hand.

His face lit up like a Christmas tree.

A Brave Act

Typical plot: witnessing a sudden emergency (e.g., a small child wandering near a busy road, a fire starting, or someone drowning) and overcoming fear to take action.

My heart hammered violently against my ribs.

Adrenaline surged through my veins as I sprang into action.

Fear temporarily paralysed me, but courage took over.

🔍3. The "Secret Weapon" Self-Check

Before you hand in your Paper 1 script, spend two minutes scanning your writing for these three critical traps that separate an AL2/3 paper from an AL1 paper:

Tense Consistency Check

P4 stories must be written entirely in the Past Tense (e.g., walked, noticed, thought). Watch out for accidental jumps into the present tense during fast-paced action scenes.

The "One-Picture" Focus Rule

You do not need to give equal weight to all three pictures. Pick one main picture to serve as your massive, detailed Climax, and use the other pictures simply to support your Exposition or Resolution.

Dialogue Formatting

Use dialogue sparingly to move the plot forward, and never use the word "said" more than twice. Replace it with descriptive verbs like muttered, stammered, bellowed, or exclaimed.

🎬5. Show, Don't Tell

Examiners reward feelings that are shown through actions and physical reactions, not just named.

Don't write "I was scared." — write "My heart pounded wildly and my palms became sweaty."

Don't write "She was happy." — write "She beamed from ear to ear."

Don't write "He was angry." — write "His face turned red and his fists were clenched tightly."

Upgrade your dialogue too

Instead of "Thank you," he said.

"Thank you so much! You really saved me!" he exclaimed gratefully.

🏁9. Universal Moral Endings

Pick one that fits your theme instead of ending abruptly.

  • I learnt that honesty is always the best policy.
  • A small act of kindness can make a big difference.
  • Teamwork helps us overcome challenges.
  • We should always help those in need.
  • Mistakes help us become better people.
  • Courage is doing the right thing even when we are afraid.

✔️10. Exam Marker's Checklist

Before handing in, ask yourself:

  • Capital letters correct?
  • Full stops and paragraphs in place?
  • Interesting verbs used instead of basic ones?
  • Feelings shown, not just named?
  • At least one line of dialogue included?
  • One or more of the five senses included?
  • A clear moral or reflection at the end?
